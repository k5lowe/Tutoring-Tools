@ Geometry > Congruence > Criteria | d2 | tags: congruence, triangles
Q: Two triangles have two pairs of equal angles and the pair of sides between those angles equal. Which congruence criterion applies, and are the triangles congruent?
A: ASA. Yes, they are congruent.
S: Angle-Side-Angle: two angles and the included side determine a triangle completely. Once two angles are fixed the third follows from the angle sum, so the shape is fixed; the given side then fixes the size. Note that the side must be the one *between* the two angles for ASA — if it is not, the criterion is AAS, which also gives congruence but by a slightly different argument.

@ Geometry > Congruence > Two-column proof | d4 | tags: congruence, proof, triangles
Q: In quadrilateral $ABCD$, $AB = CD$ and $AB \parallel CD$. Prove that triangles $ABC$ and $CDA$ are congruent.
A: By SAS: $AB = CD$ (given), $\angle BAC = \angle DCA$ (alternate angles, $AB \parallel CD$), and $AC = CA$ (common side).
S: Draw the diagonal $AC$, which both triangles contain. The three matching parts are: $AB = CD$, which is given; $\angle BAC = \angle DCA$, alternate angles cut from the parallel lines $AB$ and $CD$ by the transversal $AC$; and $AC = CA$, the shared side, which is the step students most often forget to write down. The equal angle lies between the two equal sides in each triangle, so the criterion is SAS. It follows by CPCTC that $BC = DA$, which is how one proves that a quadrilateral with one pair of sides both equal and parallel must be a parallelogram.

@ Geometry > Quadrilaterals > Parallelogram properties | d3 | tags: quadrilaterals, proof
Q: The diagonals of a quadrilateral bisect each other. Prove that it is a parallelogram.
A: The diagonals split it into two pairs of congruent triangles by SAS, and CPCTC then gives equal alternate angles, so both pairs of opposite sides are parallel.
S: Let the quadrilateral be $ABCD$ with diagonals meeting at $M$, where $AM = MC$ and $BM = MD$. Compare triangles $AMB$ and $CMD$: $AM = CM$ and $BM = DM$ are given, and $\angle AMB = \angle CMD$ because they are vertically opposite. That is SAS, so the triangles are congruent, and by CPCTC $\angle MAB = \angle MCD$. Those are alternate angles for the lines $AB$ and $CD$ cut by $AC$, so $AB \parallel CD$. @ Geometry > Quadrilaterals > Rhombus and rectangle | d3 | tags: quadrilaterals, diagonals, proof
Q: Explain why the diagonals of a rectangle are equal but the diagonals of a general parallelogram are not.
A: In a rectangle the two triangles formed by a diagonal and two sides are congruent by SAS with a right angle included, forcing equal diagonals; in a general parallelogram the included angles differ, so the diagonals differ.
S: Take parallelogram $ABCD$. Triangles $ABC$ and $BAD$ have $AB$ common, $BC = AD$ (opposite sides), and included angles $\angle ABC$ and $\angle BAD$. Q: Find the point that divides the segment from $({{x1}}, {{y1}})$ to $({{x2}}, {{y2}})$ in the ratio ${{m}} : {{n}}$, measured from the first point.

Q: Reflect the point $({{x}}, {{y}})$ in the line $y = x$, then in the $x$-axis. Give the final image.

S: Reflecting in $y = x$ swaps the coordinates, sending $({{x}}, {{y}})$ to $({{y}}, {{x}})$. Reflecting that in the $x$-axis negates the second coordinate, giving $({{y}}, {{-x}})$. Q: A dilation centred at $({{p}}, {{q}})$ with scale factor ${{k}}$ maps $({{x}}, {{y}})$ to what point?
A: $({{p + k*(x - p)}}, {{q + k*(y - q)}})$
S: A dilation stretches every point away from the centre by the scale factor, so work with the displacement from the centre rather than with the coordinates directly. The displacement is $\langle {{x - p}}, {{y - q}} \rangle$; multiplying by ${{k}}$ gives $\langle {{k*(x - p)}}, {{k*(y - q)}} \rangle$; adding that back to the centre gives $({{p + k*(x - p)}}, {{q + k*(y - q)}})$. The centre is the one point that does not move, and lengths scale by ${{k}}$ while angles are unchanged — a dilation produces a similar figure, not a congruent one.

Q: A cylinder has radius ${{r}}$ cm and height ${{h}}$ cm. Find its volume and total surface area, both exactly in terms of $\pi$.

Together that is ${{2*r*r + 2*r*h}}\pi$ cm$^2$. Unrolling the curved surface is worth doing once — it explains a formula that is otherwise just something to memorise.

@ Geometry > Surface Area and Volume > Pyramids | d3 | tags: volume, solids
Q: A square-based pyramid has base edge ${{a}}$ cm and height ${{h}}$ cm. Find its volume, and the slant height of a triangular face to two decimal places.
A: Volume ${{frac(a*a*h, 3)}}$ cm$^3$, slant height ${{fmt(sqrt(h*h + a*a/4), 2)}}$ cm
S: A pyramid has one third the volume of the prism on the same base with the same height, so the volume is $\tfrac13 ({{a}})^2({{h}}) = {{frac(a*a*h, 3)}}$ cm$^3$. For the slant height, take the right triangle formed by the vertical height, the slant height of a face, and the horizontal distance from the centre of the base to the midpoint of a base edge — that distance is half an edge, $\dfrac{ {{a}} }{2}$. Pythagoras gives $\sqrt{ {{h}}^2 + \left(\dfrac{ {{a}} }{2}\right)^2 } \approx {{fmt(sqrt(h*h + a*a/4), 2)}}$ cm. Do not confuse it with the lateral edge, which runs to a base *corner* and is longer.

Q: A cone has radius ${{6*m*n}}$ cm and vertical height ${{3*(m*m - n*n)}}$ cm. Find its slant height and its curved surface area exactly in terms of $\pi$.
A: Slant height ${{3*(m*m + n*n)}}$ cm, curved surface area ${{18*m*n*(m*m + n*n)}}\pi$ cm$^2$
S: The radius, the vertical height and the slant height form a right triangle, so the slant height is $\sqrt{ {{6*m*n}}^2 + {{3*(m*m - n*n)}}^2 } = {{3*(m*m + n*n)}}$ cm — the three lengths are a Pythagorean triple, which is why it comes out whole. The curved surface area is $\pi r l = \pi({{6*m*n}})({{3*(m*m + n*n)}}) = {{18*m*n*(m*m + n*n)}}\pi$ cm$^2$. Note that $\pi r l$ uses the slant height, not the vertical height; substituting the wrong one is the standard error here.

A: ${{frac(2*r*r*r + 3*r*r*h, 3)}}\pi$ cm$^3$
S: Break the solid into pieces whose formulas you know, then add. The cylinder contributes $\pi({{r}})^2({{h}}) = {{r*r*h}}\pi$ cm$^3$. A hemisphere is half a sphere, so it contributes $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac43 \pi ({{r}})^3 = \tfrac23 \pi ({{r*r*r}}) = \dfrac{ {{2*r*r*r}} }{3}\pi$ cm$^3$. Adding over a common denominator gives ${{frac(2*r*r*r + 3*r*r*h, 3)}}\pi$ cm$^3$. Q: A model is built to a scale of $1 : {{k}}$. The model has volume ${{v}}$ cm$^3$ and surface area ${{s}}$ cm$^2$. Find the volume and surface area of the real object.
A: Volume ${{v*k*k*k}}$ cm$^3$, surface area ${{s*k*k}}$ cm$^2$
S: Lengths scale by ${{k}}$, areas by ${{k}}^2 = {{k*k}}$ and volumes by ${{k}}^3 = {{k*k*k}}$. So the real volume is ${{v}} \times {{k*k*k}} = {{v*k*k*k}}$ cm$^3$ and the real surface area is ${{s}} \times {{k*k}} = {{s*k*k}}$ cm$^2$. A: Radius ${{r}}$, volume ${{frac(r*r*h, 3)}}\pi$
S: The small cone above the cut is similar to the whole cone, because a parallel cut preserves the shape. The cut is halfway up, so the scale factor is $\tfrac12$ and the radius halves to ${{r}}$. It comes from applying Pythagoras twice: once in the horizontal plane to get the ground distance, then again with the vertical rise.
