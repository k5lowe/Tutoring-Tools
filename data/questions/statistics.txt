@ Statistics > Probability > Without replacement | d4 | tags: probability
Q: A bag holds ${{r}}$ red counters and ${{b}}$ blue. Two are drawn without replacement. Find the probability both are red.
A: ${{frac(r*(r - 1), (r + b)*(r + b - 1))}}$
S: The first is red with probability $\dfrac{ {{r}} }{ {{r + b}} }$. With one red gone, the second is red with probability $\dfrac{ {{r - 1}} }{ {{r + b - 1}} }$. Multiplying: ${{frac(r*(r - 1), (r + b)*(r + b - 1))}}$. The denominator shrinks too — that is what "without replacement" means.

@ Statistics > Data > Effect of an outlier | d4 | tags: averages
Q: A set of ${{n}}$ values has mean ${{m}}$. One more value, ${{x}}$, is added. What is the new mean, to two decimal places?
A: ${{fmt((n*m + x) / (n + 1), 2)}}$
S: The original total is ${{n}} \times {{m}} = {{n*m}}$. Adding ${{x}}$ gives ${{n*m + x}}$ over ${{n + 1}}$ values: ${{fmt((n*m + x) / (n + 1), 2)}}$. A single extreme value can move the mean a long way, which is why the median is often reported alongside it.
V: n = int 5..20
V: m = int 10..50
V: x = int 100..300
