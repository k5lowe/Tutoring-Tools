Q: For the data ${{m - 2*k}}$, ${{m - k}}$, ${{m}}$, ${{m + k}}$, ${{m + 2*k}}$, find the population standard deviation and the sample standard deviation, both exactly.

S: Both start the same way. The data are symmetric about ${{m}}$, so the mean is ${{m}}$, and the squared deviations are ${{4*k*k}}$, ${{k*k}}$, $0$, ${{k*k}}$, ${{4*k*k}}$, summing to ${{10*k*k}}$. S: For a binomial distribution the mean is $np = {{n}} \times \dfrac{1}{ {{d}} } = {{frac(n, d)}}$, which matches intuition — in ${{n}} $ trials you expect that fraction of them to succeed. The variance is $np(1 - p) = {{n}} \times \dfrac{1}{ {{d}} } \times \dfrac{ {{d - 1}} }{ {{d}} } = {{frac(n*(d - 1), d*d)}}$. Note the variance is largest when $p = \tfrac12$ and shrinks towards zero as $p$ approaches $0$ or $1$: an outcome that is nearly certain, either way, has little left to vary.
V: n = int 8..40
V: d = int 2..6

Q: A bag has ${{r}}$ red and ${{b}}$ blue counters. Two are drawn without replacement. Find the probability that exactly one is red, as a fraction.
A: ${{frac(2*r*b, (r + b)*(r + b - 1))}}$
S: "Exactly one" needs two branches of the tree, not one, and this is where a diagram earns its place. Red then blue has probability $\dfrac{ {{r}} }{ {{r + b}} } \times \dfrac{ {{b}} }{ {{r + b - 1}} }$, and blue then red has $\dfrac{ {{b}} }{ {{r + b}} } \times \dfrac{ {{r}} }{ {{r + b - 1}} }$. The two products are equal — order does not change the chance of one of each — so the total is twice either one: $\dfrac{ {{2*r*b}} }{ {{(r + b)*(r + b - 1)}} } = {{frac(2*r*b, (r + b)*(r + b - 1))}}$. Multiply *along* branches and add *across* them: that pair of rules is the whole method.

@ Statistics > Probability > The addition rule | d3 | tags: probability
Q: For two events, $P(A) = \dfrac{ {{a}} }{ {{n}} }$, $P(B) = \dfrac{ {{b}} }{ {{n}} }$ and $P(A \cap B) = \dfrac{ {{c}} }{ {{n}} }$. Find $P(A \cup B)$.
A: $\dfrac{ {{a + b - c}} }{ {{n}} }$
S: The addition rule is $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$. Adding the two probabilities counts the overlap twice — once inside $A$ and once inside $B$ — so it has to be subtracted back off exactly once: $\dfrac{ {{a}} + {{b}} - {{c}} }{ {{n}} } = \dfrac{ {{a + b - c}} }{ {{n}} }$. Only when the events are mutually exclusive does the overlap vanish and the rule reduce to plain addition.

Q: $P(A) = \dfrac{ {{a}} }{ {{n}} }$, $P(B) = \dfrac{ {{b}} }{ {{n}} }$ and $P(A \cap B) = \dfrac{ {{a*b}} }{ {{n*n}} }$. Are $A$ and $B$ independent? Justify your answer.
A: Yes. $P(A)P(B) = \dfrac{ {{a*b}} }{ {{n*n}} }$, which equals $P(A \cap B)$.
S: Independence is defined by a calculation, not by intuition: $A$ and $B$ are independent exactly when $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$. Multiplying gives $\dfrac{ {{a}} }{ {{n}} } \times \dfrac{ {{b}} }{ {{n}} } = \dfrac{ {{a*b}} }{ {{n*n}} }$, which is what the intersection was given as, so the events are independent. Independence is a different idea from mutual exclusivity — in fact two events with non-zero probability cannot be both, since mutually exclusive events have $P(A \cap B) = 0$ while independent ones do not.

Q: Of ${{a + b + c + d}}$ people, ${{a + b}}$ own a car, and of those ${{a}}$ also own a bicycle. Find the probability that a randomly chosen car owner also owns a bicycle.
A: $\dfrac{ {{a}} }{ {{a + b}} }$
S: The phrase "a randomly chosen car owner" narrows the sample space from everyone to the ${{a + b}}$ car owners. Of those, ${{a}}$ own a bicycle, so the probability is $\dfrac{ {{a}} }{ {{a + b}} }$. This is $P(\text{bike} \mid \text{car}) = \dfrac{P(\text{bike and car})}{P(\text{car})}$, and the ${{a + b + c + d}}$ total cancels from the numerator and denominator — which is why conditional probability from a table is usually just a matter of reading the right row.

@ Statistics > Probability > Bayes and reversed conditions | d5 | tags: probability
Q: ${{p}}\%$ of a population has a condition. A test is correct ${{q}}\%$ of the time for those who have it, and gives a false positive for ${{f}}\%$ of those who do not. Out of ${{10000}}$ people, how many test positive, and what fraction of those actually have the condition?
A: ${{p*q + (100 - p)*f}}$ test positive, of whom $\dfrac{ {{p*q}} }{ {{p*q + (100 - p)*f}} }$ have the condition.
S: Work with counts rather than probabilities and the arithmetic stays concrete. Of $10000$ people, ${{100*p}}$ have the condition and ${{100*(100 - p)}}$ do not. True positives: ${{q}}\%$ of ${{100*p}}$ is ${{p*q}}$. False positives: ${{f}}\%$ of ${{100*(100 - p)}}$ is ${{(100 - p)*f}}$. So ${{p*q + (100 - p)*f}}$ test positive in total, and the fraction who genuinely have it is $\dfrac{ {{p*q}} }{ {{p*q + (100 - p)*f}} }$. The lesson is that a test being ${{q}}\%$ accurate does not mean a positive result is ${{q}}\%$ reliable: when the condition is rare, the false positives are drawn from a much larger group and can easily outnumber the true ones.

@ Statistics > Counting > Arrangements with repeated letters | d4 | tags: counting, permutations
Q: How many distinct arrangements are there of the letters in a word of ${{n}}$ letters, where one letter appears ${{r}}$ times and the rest are all different?
A: ${{frac(fact(n), fact(r))}}$
S: If all ${{n}}$ letters were different there would be ${{n}}! = {{fact(n)}}$ arrangements. But the ${{r}}$ copies of the repeated letter are indistinguishable, so every distinct arrangement has been counted ${{r}}! = {{fact(r)}}$ times, once for each way of permuting those identical copies among themselves. Dividing gives $\dfrac{ {{fact(n)}} }{ {{fact(r)}} } = {{frac(fact(n), fact(r))}}$. The same correction applies once per repeated letter, dividing by the factorial of each group's size.

@ Statistics > Counting > Permutations and combinations | d3 | tags: counting
Q: From ${{n}}$ people, how many ways are there to choose a committee of ${{k}}$, and how many ways to choose a president, secretary and treasurer if ${{k}} = 3$?
A: $\binom{ {{n}} }{ {{k}} } = {{nCr(n, k)}}$ committees; ${{nPr(n, 3)}}$ ways to fill the three named posts.
S: The difference is whether order matters. A committee is just a set of people, so choosing $\{A, B, C\}$ is the same committee however you list them: that is a combination, $\binom{ {{n}} }{ {{k}} } = {{nCr(n, k)}}$. Named posts are different — making $A$ president and $B$ secretary is not the same as the reverse — so that is a permutation, ${{n}}P_3 = {{nPr(n, 3)}}$. The two are related by a factor of $3! = 6$: every committee of three can be arranged into the three posts in $6$ ways.

@ Statistics > Counting > Complementary counting | d4 | tags: counting, probability
Q: ${{n}}$ fair dice are rolled. Find the probability that at least one shows a six, as a fraction.
A: $\dfrac{ {{6^n - 5^n}} }{ {{6^n}} }$
S: Counting the ways to get at least one six directly means handling one six, two sixes and so on, with overlaps to worry about. The complement is far simpler: *no* sixes means every die shows one of the other five faces, which happens in $5^{ {{n}} } = {{5^n}}$ of the $6^{ {{n}} } = {{6^n}}$ equally likely outcomes. So at least one six occurs in ${{6^n}} - {{5^n}} = {{6^n - 5^n}}$ outcomes, giving $\dfrac{ {{6^n - 5^n}} }{ {{6^n}} }$. Any time a question says "at least one", the complement is worth trying first.

@ Statistics > Sampling > Bias | d3 | tags: sampling
Q: A survey about library funding is conducted by asking people as they leave the library. Explain why this sample is biased, and name the type of bias.
A: People in the library use it more than the general population does, so they will over-report support. This is selection bias.
S: The sampling method itself decides who can appear in the sample, and here only library users can. Those people are, almost by definition, more favourable towards library funding than the population the survey claims to describe. That is selection bias: the frame from which the sample is drawn does not match the population of interest. Note that increasing the sample size does nothing to fix it — asking a thousand people leaving the library gives a more precise estimate of the wrong quantity.

@ Statistics > Sampling > Distribution of the sample mean | d4 | tags: sampling, normal-distribution
Q: A population has mean ${{m}}$ and standard deviation ${{s}}$. Samples of size ${{n*n}}$ are taken. Find the mean and standard error of the sample mean.
A: Mean ${{m}}$, standard error ${{frac(s, n)}}$
S: The sample mean is centred on the population mean, so its expected value is ${{m}}$ — sampling does not systematically push the estimate up or down. Its standard deviation, called the standard error, is $\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \dfrac{ {{s}} }{\sqrt{ {{n*n}} }} = \dfrac{ {{s}} }{ {{n}} } = {{frac(s, n)}}$. The square root is the important part: quartering the standard error needs sixteen times the sample, not four, which is why very precise surveys are expensive.

Q: A sample of ${{n*n}}$ observations has mean ${{m}}$, from a population with standard deviation ${{s}}$. Construct an approximate $95\%$ confidence interval for the population mean.
A: ${{m}} \pm {{fmt(1.96*s/n, 2)}}$, that is $({{fmt(m - 1.96*s/n, 2)}}, {{fmt(m + 1.96*s/n, 2)}})$
S: The standard error is $\dfrac{ {{s}} }{\sqrt{ {{n*n}} }} = {{frac(s, n)}}$. A $95\%$ interval reaches $1.96$ standard errors either side of the sample mean, giving a margin of error of $1.96 \times {{frac(s, n)}} \approx {{fmt(1.96*s/n, 2)}}$. So the interval is $({{fmt(m - 1.96*s/n, 2)}}, {{fmt(m + 1.96*s/n, 2)}})$. @ Statistics > Inference > Interpreting a confidence interval | d4 | tags: inference, confidence-intervals
Q: A $95\%$ confidence interval for a population mean is $({{a}}, {{b}})$. Is it correct to say there is a $95\%$ probability the population mean lies between ${{a}}$ and ${{b}}$? Explain.
A: No. The population mean is a fixed number, not random. The $95\%$ describes the method: intervals built this way capture the true mean $95\%$ of the time.
S: The population mean is a fixed if unknown constant, so it either is or is not between ${{a}}$ and ${{b}}$ — there is no probability about it once the interval is calculated. What is random is the *interval*, which depends on which sample happened to be drawn. The correct reading is about the procedure: if many samples were taken and an interval built from each in the same way, about $95\%$ of those intervals would contain the true mean. This particular one either does or does not, and you cannot tell which.

Q: A $95\%$ confidence interval for a mean has margin of error ${{e}}$ with a sample of size ${{n}}$. Roughly what sample size would be needed to halve the margin of error?
A: About ${{4*n}}$
S: The margin of error is $1.96 \times \dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$, so it is inversely proportional to $\sqrt{n}$. Halving it means doubling $\sqrt{n}$, which means multiplying $n$ by $4$: about $4 \times {{n}} = {{4*n}}$ observations. @ Statistics > Inference > Hypothesis test setup | d4 | tags: inference, hypothesis-testing
Q: A manufacturer claims its bags contain ${{m}}$ g on average. A consumer group suspects the bags are underfilled. State the null and alternative hypotheses.
A: $H_0: \mu = {{m}}$ against $H_1: \mu < {{m}}$
S: The null hypothesis is always the claim of no effect or no difference — here, that the manufacturer is right and the mean really is ${{m}}$ g. The alternative captures what the investigator suspects, and the wording decides its direction: "underfilled" points one way only, so the test is one-tailed with $H_1: \mu < {{m}}$. Had the group merely suspected the mean was wrong, the alternative would be $\mu \ne {{m}}$ and the test two-tailed. Note that hypotheses are always about the population parameter $\mu$, never about the sample mean, which is known.

@ Statistics > Inference > Interpreting a p-value | d4 | tags: inference, hypothesis-testing
Q: A test of $H_0: \mu = {{m}}$ gives $p = 0.0{{p}}$. At the $5\%$ significance level, state the conclusion and say what the $p$-value means.
A: {{ifelse(p < 5, 'Reject', 'Do not reject')}} $H_0$. The $p$-value is the probability of a result at least as extreme as the one observed, if $H_0$ were true.
S: Compare the $p$-value with the significance level: $0.0{{p}}$ is {{ifelse(p < 5, 'below', 'above')}} $0.05$, so {{ifelse(p < 5, 'reject $H_0$ — the data are unlikely enough under the null hypothesis to count as evidence against it', 'do not reject $H_0$ — the data are not surprising enough under the null hypothesis to count as evidence against it')}}. @ Statistics > Inference > Type I and Type II errors | d4 | tags: inference, hypothesis-testing
Q: In a test of whether a new drug works, describe a Type I error and a Type II error in context.
A: Type I: concluding the drug works when it does not. Type II: concluding it does not work when it does.
S: A Type I error rejects a true null hypothesis — here the null is that the drug has no effect, so a Type I error means announcing an effect that is not there, a false positive. Its probability is the significance level you chose. A Type II error fails to reject a false null: the drug does work, but the study missed it, a false negative. Lowering the significance level guards against Type I errors but makes Type II errors more likely, which is why the choice depends on which mistake costs more — approving a useless drug, or shelving a useful one.

@ Statistics > Regression > Extrapolation and causation | d4 | tags: regression, correlation
Q: Ice cream sales and drowning deaths are strongly positively correlated across the year. Does this show that ice cream causes drownings? What is the likely explanation?
A: No. A third variable — hot weather — raises both. Correlation does not establish causation.
S: Two variables can move together for several reasons: one may cause the other, the causation may run the other way, or both may be driven by something else. Here the third variable is temperature: hot weather sells ice cream and also sends people swimming, where more drownings occur. Neither of the two measured variables affects the other at all. Establishing causation needs more than a correlation — ideally a controlled experiment, where the suspected cause is assigned rather than merely observed.

Q: In a cumulative frequency table of ${{4*n}}$ observations, at what cumulative frequencies do you read off the median and the two quartiles?
A: Median at ${{2*n}}$, $Q_1$ at ${{n}}$, $Q_3$ at ${{3*n}}$
S: A cumulative frequency graph gives positions, so read across at the fraction of the total you want. The median is halfway through the data, at $\dfrac{ {{4*n}} }{2} = {{2*n}}$; the lower quartile is a quarter of the way, at ${{n}}$; the upper quartile three quarters, at ${{3*n}}$.
