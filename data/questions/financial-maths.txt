@ Financial Maths > Percentages > Successive changes | d2 | tags: percentages, drill

Q: A price of $\${{p}}$ rises by ${{a}}\%$ and then falls by ${{b}}\%$. Find the final price and the overall percentage change, to two decimal places.
A: $\${{fmt(p*(1 + a/100)*(1 - b/100), 2)}}$, an overall change of ${{fmt(((1 + a/100)*(1 - b/100) - 1)*100, 2)}}\%$
S: Each change multiplies by a factor: a rise of ${{a}}\%$ multiplies by ${{fmt(1 + a/100, 4)}}$, and a fall of ${{b}}\%$ multiplies by ${{fmt(1 - b/100, 4)}}$. Applying both gives $\${{p}} \times {{fmt(1 + a/100, 4)}} \times {{fmt(1 - b/100, 4)}} = \${{fmt(p*(1 + a/100)*(1 - b/100), 2)}}$. The overall factor is ${{fmt((1 + a/100)*(1 - b/100), 6)}}$, so the overall change is ${{fmt(((1 + a/100)*(1 - b/100) - 1)*100, 2)}}\%$ — not ${{a - b}}\%$. Percentage changes multiply rather than add, because the second one is taken on the already-changed amount. A rise of $25\%$ followed by a fall of $20\%$ returns you exactly to where you started, which is the cleanest illustration of the point.
V: p = int 40..400 step 5
V: a = int 5..40
V: b = int 5..40
C: a != b

Q: An interest rate rises from ${{a}}\%$ to ${{b}}\%$. By how many percentage points has it risen, and by what percentage has it risen?
A: ${{b - a}}$ percentage point{{ifelse(b - a == 1, '', 's')}}, which is a rise of ${{fmt((b - a)/a*100, 2)}}\%$
S: The two are different measurements of the same move. Subtracting gives the change in *percentage points*: ${{b}} - {{a}} = {{b - a}}$. Dividing by the starting value gives the *percentage* change: $\dfrac{ {{b - a}} }{ {{a}} } \times 100 = {{fmt((b - a)/a*100, 2)}}\%$. Both are correct and they mean different things, which is why the phrase "percentage points" exists at all — reporting this rise as "${{b - a}}\%$" would understate it badly, and reporting it as "${{fmt((b - a)/a*100, 2)}}$ percentage points" would overstate it just as badly.

Q: A shop buys an item for $\${{c}}$ and sells it for $\${{s}}$. Find the mark-up as a percentage of cost, and the profit margin as a percentage of the selling price.
A: Mark-up ${{fmt((s - c)/c*100, 2)}}\%$, margin ${{fmt((s - c)/s*100, 2)}}\%$
S: The profit is $\${{s}} - \${{c}} = \${{s - c}}$ either way; what changes is what you divide by. Mark-up is measured against cost: $\dfrac{ {{s - c}} }{ {{c}} } \times 100 = {{fmt((s - c)/c*100, 2)}}\%$. Margin is measured against the selling price: $\dfrac{ {{s - c}} }{ {{s}} } \times 100 = {{fmt((s - c)/s*100, 2)}}\%$. The margin is always the smaller of the two, since the selling price is the larger denominator. Confusing them is a genuinely expensive mistake in retail: a $50\%$ mark-up is only a $33.3\%$ margin.

Q: $\${{p}}$ is invested at a simple interest rate of ${{r}}\%$ per year for ${{t}}$ years. How much interest is earned, and what is the final balance?
A: Interest $\${{fmt(p*r*t/100, 2)}}$, balance $\${{fmt(p + p*r*t/100, 2)}}$
S: Simple interest is charged on the original amount every year and never on the interest itself, so $I = Prt$ with $r$ as a decimal: $I = {{p}} \times {{fmt(r/100, 4)}} \times {{t}} = \${{fmt(p*r*t/100, 2)}}$. The balance is the principal plus that interest, $\${{p}} + \${{fmt(p*r*t/100, 2)}} = \${{fmt(p + p*r*t/100, 2)}}$. Each year earns exactly $\${{fmt(p*r/100, 2)}}$ — the same amount every year, which is what "simple" means and what separates it from compound interest.

It is worth dividing by the number of years before reading off a rate: the total return over the whole term here is ${{fmt(r*t, 2)}}\%$, and quoting that as though it were annual is how a modest investment gets advertised as a spectacular one.

Q: A nominal rate of ${{r}}\%$ per year is compounded ${{n}}$ times a year. Find the effective annual rate, to three decimal places.
A: ${{fmt(((1 + r/(100*n))^n - 1)*100, 3)}}\%$
S: The effective annual rate is the single annual rate that would produce the same growth in one year. One year of compounding multiplies the balance by $\left(1 + {{fmt(r/(100*n), 6)}}\right)^{ {{n}} } = {{fmt((1 + r/(100*n))^n, 6)}}$, so the effective rate is ${{fmt(((1 + r/(100*n))^n - 1)*100, 3)}}\%$ — above the nominal ${{r}}\%$, and that gap is the whole point of the figure. It is the only fair way to compare two accounts quoting different compounding frequencies, and it is why lenders are required to publish it.

Q: Income tax is charged at $0\%$ on the first $\${{t1}}$, ${{r1}}\%$ on income between $\${{t1}}$ and $\${{t2}}$, and ${{r2}}\%$ on income above $\${{t2}}$. For an income of $\${{y}}$, find the tax owed, the marginal rate and the average rate.
A: Tax $\${{fmt(r1/100*(t2 - t1) + r2/100*(y - t2), 2)}}$, marginal rate ${{r2}}\%$, average rate ${{fmt((r1/100*(t2 - t1) + r2/100*(y - t2))/y*100, 2)}}\%$
S: Tax each band separately. The first $\${{t1}}$ is untaxed. The band from $\${{t1}}$ to $\${{t2}}$ is $\${{t2 - t1}}$ wide and taxed at ${{r1}}\%$: $\${{fmt(r1/100*(t2 - t1), 2)}}$. The income above $\${{t2}}$ is $\${{y - t2}}$, taxed at ${{r2}}\%$: $\${{fmt(r2/100*(y - t2), 2)}}$. The total is $\${{fmt(r1/100*(t2 - t1) + r2/100*(y - t2), 2)}}$. The marginal rate — what the *next* dollar earned would be taxed at — is ${{r2}}\%$, while the average rate is the total tax over the whole income, ${{fmt((r1/100*(t2 - t1) + r2/100*(y - t2))/y*100, 2)}}\%$. The average is always the lower of the two under a progressive system, since the earlier bands are taxed more lightly. Applying ${{r2}}\%$ to the entire income would give $\${{fmt(r2/100*y, 2)}}$, and the belief that a raise into a higher bracket can leave you worse off comes from exactly that error.
V: t1 = int 10000..20000 step 1000
V: t2 = int 40000..70000 step 5000
V: r1 = choice 15, 19, 20, 25
V: r2 = choice 30, 32, 35, 37, 40
V: y = int 75000..200000 step 5000
C: r2 > r1

@ Financial Maths > Shares and Business > Dividend yield | d3 | tags: shares, percentages
Q: An investor buys ${{n}}$ shares at $\${{fmt(p, 2)}}$ each. The company pays a dividend of ${{fmt(d, 2)}}$ cents per share. Find the total dividend and the dividend yield, to two decimal places.
A: Total dividend $\${{fmt(n*d/100, 2)}}$, a yield of ${{fmt(d/p, 2)}}\%$
S: The dividend per share is ${{fmt(d, 2)}}$ cents, or $\${{fmt(d/100, 4)}}$, so ${{n}}$ shares pay ${{n}} \times \${{fmt(d/100, 4)}} = \${{fmt(n*d/100, 2)}}$. The yield expresses that as a percentage of what the shares cost: $\dfrac{ {{fmt(d/100, 4)}} }{ {{fmt(p, 2)}} } \times 100 = {{fmt(d/p, 2)}}\%$. Note the share count cancels — the yield is a property of the share price and the dividend, not of how many you own. It is the figure to set against an interest rate when comparing shares with a savings account, though unlike interest it is not promised.
V: n = int 100..5000 step 50
V: p = decimal 2..60 step 0.01
V: d = decimal 5..200 step 0.5

@ Financial Maths > Shares and Business > Break-even | d3 | tags: business
Q: A business has fixed costs of $\${{f}}$ a month. Each unit costs $\${{fmt(c, 2)}}$ to make and sells for $\${{fmt(s, 2)}}$. How many units must be sold each month to break even, and what is the profit on ${{n}}$ units?
A: ${{ceil(f/(s - c))}}$ units; the profit on ${{n}}$ units is $\${{fmt(n*(s - c) - f, 2)}}$
S: Each unit sold contributes $\${{fmt(s, 2)}} - \${{fmt(c, 2)}} = \${{fmt(s - c, 2)}}$ towards the fixed costs — the contribution margin. Break-even is where the contributions cover the fixed costs: $\${{f}} \div \${{fmt(s - c, 2)}} = {{fmt(f/(s - c), 2)}}$, so ${{ceil(f/(s - c))}}$ units, rounded up because a part unit does not sell. Profit on ${{n}}$ units is ${{n}} \times \${{fmt(s - c, 2)}} - \${{f}} = \${{fmt(n*(s - c) - f, 2)}}$. The selling price and unit cost matter only through their difference; a business with a thin margin needs a great many sales before the fixed costs are covered at all.
V: f = int 2000..30000 step 500
V: c = decimal 3..40 step 0.5
V: s = decimal 5..90 step 0.5
V: n = int 500..5000 step 50
C: s > c*1.25
C: n*(s - c) > f*1.1

@ Financial Maths > Shares and Business > Leasing against buying | d4 | tags: business, comparison
Q: A machine can be bought for $\${{p}}$ and sold after ${{y}}$ years for ${{q}}\%$ of its purchase price, or leased for $\${{m}}$ a month. Ignoring interest, which is cheaper over ${{y}}$ years, and by how much?
A: {{ifelse(p*(1 - q/100) < 12*y*m, 'Buying', 'Leasing')}}, by $\${{fmt(abs(p*(1 - q/100) - 12*y*m), 2)}}$
S: Buying costs the purchase price less whatever comes back on resale: $\${{p}} - {{q}}\%$ of $\${{p}} = \${{p}} - \${{fmt(p*q/100, 2)}} = \${{fmt(p*(1 - q/100), 2)}}$. Leasing costs $12 \times {{y}} \times \${{m}} = \${{fmt(12*y*m, 2)}}$. So {{ifelse(p*(1 - q/100) < 12*y*m, 'buying', 'leasing')}} is cheaper by $\${{fmt(abs(p*(1 - q/100) - 12*y*m), 2)}}$. The comparison turns on the resale value, which is a forecast rather than a known number — and on the instruction to ignore interest, which flatters buying, since the purchase ties up $\${{p}}$ on day one while the lease payments are spread over ${{12*y}}$ months.
V: p = int 20000..150000 step 5000
V: q = int 10..50
V: y = choice 3, 4, 5, 6
V: m = int 300..3000 step 50
C: abs(p*(1 - q/100) - 12*y*m) > 500

@ Financial Maths > Shares and Business > Saving for a deposit | d5 | tags: annuities, savings
Q: A couple want a $\${{d}}$ deposit in ${{y}}$ years. They have $\${{p}}$ now in an account paying ${{r}}\%$ per year compounded monthly, and will add the same amount at the end of each month. How much must they add each month?
A: $\${{fmt((d - p*(1 + i)^(12*y))*i/((1 + i)^(12*y) - 1), 2)}}$
S: The money they already have grows on its own to ${{p}} \times {{fmt((1 + i)^(12*y), 6)}} = \${{fmt(p*(1 + i)^(12*y), 2)}}$, leaving $\${{d}} - \${{fmt(p*(1 + i)^(12*y), 2)}} = \${{fmt(d - p*(1 + i)^(12*y), 2)}}$ for the monthly deposits to cover. The future value of ${{12*y}}$ deposits of $M$ is $M\dfrac{(1 + i)^n - 1}{i}$, so $M = \dfrac{ {{fmt(d - p*(1 + i)^(12*y), 2)}} \times {{fmt(i, 8)}} }{ {{fmt((1 + i)^(12*y) - 1, 6)}} } = \${{fmt((d - p*(1 + i)^(12*y))*i/((1 + i)^(12*y) - 1), 2)}}$. Working out what the lump sum will do first, and only then sizing the deposits to the shortfall, keeps the two streams from getting tangled — the same split as valuing a lump sum and an annuity separately.
V: d = int 40000..150000 step 5000
V: p = int 2000..30000 step 1000
V: y = int 3..10
V: r = int 2..8
V: i = expr r/1200
C: d > p*3
