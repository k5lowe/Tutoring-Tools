@ Algebra 2 > Complex Numbers > Properties of the conjugate | d3 | tags: complex-numbers, conjugate, proof
Q: Prove that $\overline{z_1 + z_2} = \overline{z_1} + \overline{z_2}$ for all complex numbers $z_1$ and $z_2$.
A: Write $z_1 = a + bi$ and $z_2 = c + di$. Then $z_1 + z_2 = (a+c) + (b+d)i$, so $\overline{z_1 + z_2} = (a+c) - (b+d)i$. Separately $\overline{z_1} + \overline{z_2} = (a - bi) + (c - di) = (a+c) - (b+d)i$. The two agree.
S: The whole proof is bookkeeping on real and imaginary parts. Conjugation only flips the sign of the imaginary part, and addition handles real and imaginary parts independently, so it makes no difference whether you flip first and add, or add first and flip.

@ Algebra 2 > Complex Numbers > Properties of the conjugate | d4 | tags: complex-numbers, conjugate, proof
Q: Prove that $\overline{z_1 z_2} = \overline{z_1} \, \overline{z_2}$ for all complex numbers $z_1$ and $z_2$.
A: Write $z_1 = a + bi$ and $z_2 = c + di$. Then $z_1 z_2 = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i$, so $\overline{z_1 z_2} = (ac - bd) - (ad + bc)i$. Separately $\overline{z_1}\,\overline{z_2} = (a - bi)(c - di) = ac - adi - bci + bdi^2 = (ac - bd) - (ad + bc)i$. The two agree.
S: Notice where the minus signs come from: in the second product both imaginary parts are negated, so the cross terms $-adi$ and $-bci$ each pick up one minus sign, while the $bd$ term picks up two and is unchanged apart from $i^2 = -1$.

@ Algebra 2 > Complex Numbers > Properties of the conjugate | d5 | tags: complex-numbers, conjugate, proof, induction
Q: Prove that $\overline{z^{\,n}} = \left(\overline{z}\right)^n$ for every complex number $z$ and every positive integer $n$.
A: By induction on $n$. Base case $n = 1$: both sides are $\overline{z}$. Inductive step: suppose $\overline{z^{\,k}} = (\overline{z})^k$. Then $\overline{z^{\,k+1}} = \overline{z^{\,k} \cdot z} = \overline{z^{\,k}} \cdot \overline{z}$ by the product rule for conjugates, which equals $(\overline{z})^k \cdot \overline{z} = (\overline{z})^{k+1}$. So the statement holds for $k+1$ whenever it holds for $k$, and by induction it holds for all $n \ge 1$.
S: The only fact doing real work is $\overline{z_1 z_2} = \overline{z_1}\,\overline{z_2}$; induction just applies it $n-1$ times. @ Algebra 2 > Complex Numbers > De Moivre's theorem | d4 | tags: complex-numbers, de-moivre, euler-form, proof
Q: State De Moivre's theorem and prove it for positive integers $n$.
A: The theorem says $\left(re^{i\theta}\right)^n = r^n e^{in\theta}$, and in particular $(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta$. Proof by induction on $n$. For $n = 1$ both sides are $re^{i\theta}$. Assuming $\left(re^{i\theta}\right)^k = r^k e^{ik\theta}$, multiply both sides by $re^{i\theta}$ and apply the product rule: $\left(re^{i\theta}\right)^{k+1} = r^k e^{ik\theta} \cdot re^{i\theta} = r^{k+1}e^{i(k\theta + \theta)} = r^{k+1}e^{i(k+1)\theta}$. So it holds for $k+1$ whenever it holds for $k$.
S: The one fact doing the work is the product rule, which was itself proved from the addition formulae rather than assumed — so the argument is not circular, even though in Euler notation the conclusion looks like nothing more than the index law. De Moivre is that rule applied $n$ times to the same number: each multiplication by $z$ scales by $r$ and rotates by $\theta$, so $n$ of them scale by $r^n$ and rotate by $n\theta$. It extends to negative integers using $z^{-1} = r^{-1}e^{-i\theta}$.

Q: Can synthetic division be used to divide by ${{coef(a,'x')}} {{signed(-b)}}$, and by $x^2 {{signed(c)}}$?
A: By ${{coef(a,'x')}} {{signed(-b)}}$: yes, with care — divide by ${{frac(b, a)}}$ and then divide the resulting quotient by ${{a}}$. By $x^2 {{signed(c)}}$: no, synthetic division only handles linear divisors.
S: Synthetic division is built for a divisor of the form $x - r$. A linear divisor with a leading coefficient can be handled by factoring it as ${{a}}\left(x - {{frac(b, a)}}\right)$: run the division with the root ${{frac(b, a)}}$, then divide the quotient by ${{a}}$. The remainder needs no adjustment, since it is $p\left({{frac(b, a)}}\right)$ either way. A quadratic divisor has no such trick — the array has one column per step and cannot carry two coefficients at once, so long division is the only route.
