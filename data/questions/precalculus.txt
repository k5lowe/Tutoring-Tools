Q: Prove that $\tan\theta + \cot\theta = \sec\theta\csc\theta$.
A: Work on the left. $\tan\theta + \cot\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} + \dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} = \dfrac{\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta}{\sin\theta\cos\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\sin\theta\cos\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta} \cdot \dfrac{1}{\sin\theta} = \sec\theta\csc\theta$.

Note that the identity holds only where both sides are defined, so $\theta$ cannot be a multiple of $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$.

Q: Prove that $\dfrac{\sin\theta}{1 - \cos\theta} = \dfrac{1 + \cos\theta}{\sin\theta}$.

Q: Given the formula for $\cos(A + B)$, derive $\cos(A - B)$, $\sin(A - B)$ and $\tan(A + B)$.
A: For $\cos(A - B)$, replace $B$ by $-B$ in $\cos(A+B) = \cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B$. The same substitution in $\sin(A+B) = \sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B$ gives $\sin(A - B) = \sin A\cos B - \cos A\sin B$. For the tangent, divide: $\tan(A+B) = \dfrac{\sin(A+B)}{\cos(A+B)} = \dfrac{\sin A\cos B + \cos A\sin B}{\cos A\cos B - \sin A\sin B}$, then divide numerator and denominator by $\cos A\cos B$ to get $\dfrac{\tan A + \tan B}{1 - \tan A\tan B}$.
S: Only one formula has to be memorised — the rest follow from the odd and even symmetries and from $\tan = \dfrac{\sin}{\cos}$. The division by $\cos A\cos B$ in the last step is the standard trick for turning a sine-and-cosine expression into a tangent one; it assumes $\cos A\cos B \ne 0$, which is why $\tan(A+B)$ has more excluded angles than $\sin(A+B)$.

Q: Prove that $\sin(A + B)\sin(A - B) = \sin^2 A - \sin^2 B$.

Q: Prove that $\cos A - \cos B = -2\sin\dfrac{A+B}{2}\sin\dfrac{A-B}{2}$.

Note the minus sign on the right — a larger angle has a smaller cosine, and the formula has to record that.

Q: Prove that $\tan 3\theta = \dfrac{3\tan\theta - \tan^3\theta}{1 - 3\tan^2\theta}$.

Now substitute $\tan 2\theta = \dfrac{2t}{1 - t^2}$, writing $t$ for $\tan\theta$. The numerator becomes $\dfrac{2t}{1-t^2} + t = \dfrac{2t + t(1-t^2)}{1-t^2} = \dfrac{3t - t^3}{1-t^2}$, and the denominator becomes $1 - \dfrac{2t^2}{1-t^2} = \dfrac{(1-t^2) - 2t^2}{1-t^2} = \dfrac{1 - 3t^2}{1-t^2}$. The factors of $\dfrac{1}{1-t^2}$ cancel, leaving $\dfrac{3t - t^3}{1 - 3t^2}$.
S: Two moves carry the proof: splitting $3\theta$ as $2\theta + \theta$ so that known formulae apply, and abbreviating $\tan\theta$ to $t$ so the algebra is readable. The shared denominator $1 - t^2$ cancelling is what keeps the result tidy. Note that the excluded angles multiply here — $\theta$ must avoid making $\tan\theta$, $\tan 2\theta$ or $\tan 3\theta$ undefined, and must avoid $1 - 3\tan^2\theta = 0$.

S: Multiply both sides by $r$ to get quantities that convert cleanly: $r^2 = {{2*a}}r\cos\theta$. Now $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$ and $r\cos\theta = x$, so $x^2 + y^2 = {{2*a}}x$. Completing the square in $x$ gives $(x - {{a}})^2 + y^2 = {{a*a}}$, a circle of radius ${{a}}$ through the origin with centre $({{a}}, 0)$. Multiplying by $r$ is the standard trick — it can in principle add the origin as a spurious solution, though here the origin is genuinely on the curve, at $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$.

Q: Eliminate the parameter from $x = {{r}}\cos t$, $y = {{r}}\sin t$, and state the curve and the direction of travel as $t$ increases.
A: $x^2 + y^2 = {{r*r}}$ — a circle of radius ${{r}}$, traced anticlockwise.
S: Square and add, so that the Pythagorean identity does the work: $x^2 + y^2 = {{r*r}}\cos^2 t + {{r*r}}\sin^2 t = {{r*r}}(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t) = {{r*r}}$. That is a circle of radius ${{r}}$ centred at the origin. For the direction, follow a few values: at $t = 0$ the point is at $({{r}}, 0)$ and at $t = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ it is at $(0, {{r}})$, so it moves anticlockwise. Swapping sine and cosine would reverse that, which is exactly the kind of detail the Cartesian equation cannot record.

Q: A projectile has $x = {{u}}t$ and $y = {{v}}t - 5t^2$ metres. Find when it returns to $y = 0$ and how far it has then travelled horizontally.
A: At $t = {{frac(v, 5)}}$ s, having travelled ${{frac(u*v, 5)}}$ m
S: Set $y = 0$: $t\left({{v}} - 5t\right) = 0$, so $t = 0$ at launch and $t = {{frac(v, 5)}}$ on landing. The horizontal position is unaffected by gravity and grows steadily, so at that moment $x = {{u}} \times {{frac(v, 5)}} = {{frac(u*v, 5)}}$ m. Keeping the two coordinates as separate functions of $t$ is what makes this easy: the vertical motion has the acceleration and the horizontal motion does not, and a single Cartesian equation would tangle them together.

Q: Find the focus and directrix of the parabola $x^2 = {{4*p}}y$.
A: Focus $(0, {{p}})$; directrix $y = {{-p}}$
S: Compare with the standard form $x^2 = 4py$, which gives $4p = {{4*p}}$ and so $p = {{p}}$. For this orientation the focus sits at $(0, p) = (0, {{p}})$, inside the curve, and the directrix is the horizontal line $y = -p = {{-p}}$, the same distance on the other side of the vertex. The defining property is that every point of the parabola is equidistant from the focus and the directrix — the vertex at the origin is the obvious case, being ${{p}}$ from each.

Q: Find the vertices and the equations of the asymptotes of $\dfrac{x^2}{ {{a*a}} } - \dfrac{y^2}{ {{b*b}} } = 1$.
A: Vertices $(\pm {{a}}, 0)$; asymptotes $y = \pm\dfrac{ {{b}} }{ {{a}} }x$
S: The $x^2$ term is the positive one, so the hyperbola opens left and right, and the vertices are at $(\pm a, 0) = (\pm {{a}}, 0)$. The asymptotes come from replacing the $1$ on the right with $0$: $\dfrac{x^2}{ {{a*a}} } = \dfrac{y^2}{ {{b*b}} }$ gives $y = \pm\dfrac{ {{b}} }{ {{a}} }x$. Far from the origin the constant term matters less and less, which is why the curve settles onto these lines. Note ${{b}}$ appears in the asymptote slopes but not in the vertices — it controls how steeply the branches open, not where they start.

Q: Find the foci and the eccentricity of $\dfrac{x^2}{ {{(m*m + n*n)*(m*m + n*n)}} } + \dfrac{y^2}{ {{(2*m*n)*(2*m*n)}} } = 1$.
A: Foci $(\pm {{m*m - n*n}}, 0)$; eccentricity $\dfrac{ {{m*m - n*n}} }{ {{m*m + n*n}} }$
S: The larger denominator is under $x^2$, so the major axis is horizontal with $a = {{m*m + n*n}}$ and $b = {{2*m*n}}$. For an ellipse $c^2 = a^2 - b^2 = {{(m*m + n*n)*(m*m + n*n)}} - {{(2*m*n)*(2*m*n)}} = {{(m*m - n*n)*(m*m - n*n)}}$, so $c = {{m*m - n*n}}$ and the foci are at $(\pm {{m*m - n*n}}, 0)$, on the major axis. The eccentricity is $e = \dfrac{c}{a} = \dfrac{ {{m*m - n*n}} }{ {{m*m + n*n}} }$, a number between $0$ and $1$ measuring how far from circular the ellipse is: $e = 0$ would be a circle, and $e$ near $1$ a very elongated one. Note the minus sign — for a hyperbola the relation is $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$ instead.

@ Precalculus > Sequences and Series > Infinite geometric series | d4 | tags: sequences, series
Q: Find the sum of the infinite geometric series with first term ${{a}}$ and common ratio $\dfrac{1}{ {{r}} }$.
A: ${{frac(a*r, r - 1)}}$
S: An infinite geometric series converges exactly when $|r| < 1$, and here $\dfrac{1}{ {{r}} }$ satisfies that. The sum is $\dfrac{a_1}{1 - r} = \dfrac{ {{a}} }{1 - \frac{1}{ {{r}} }} = \dfrac{ {{a}} }{\frac{ {{r - 1}} }{ {{r}} }} = {{frac(a*r, r - 1)}}$. The convergence condition is the whole point: each term is a fixed fraction of the one before, so the terms shrink geometrically and the tail contributes ever less. With $|r| \ge 1$ the terms do not shrink and no finite sum exists.

A: $y = x {{signed(b + p)}}$
S: A rational function has a slant asymptote when the numerator's degree is exactly one more than the denominator's, as here. Divide: $x^2 {{term(b,'x')}} {{signed(c)}}$ divided by $x {{signed(-p)}}$ gives a quotient of $x {{signed(b + p)}}$ with some remainder. Q: The graph of $y = f(x)$ is transformed to $y = {{a}}f\left(x {{signed(-h)}}\right) {{signed(k)}}$. Describe the transformations in order.
A: Stretch vertically by ${{a}}$, then translate ${{h}} $ in the $x$-direction and ${{k}}$ in the $y$-direction.
S: Read the changes from the inside out. The $x {{signed(-h)}}$ inside the bracket shifts the graph ${{h}}$ to the right — inside changes act on the input and go the *opposite* way to their sign, which is the standard trap. The ${{a}}$ outside multiplies every output, stretching vertically by ${{a}}$ from the $x$-axis. The ${{signed(k)}}$ outside shifts everything ${{k}}$ vertically. Outside changes behave as written; inside changes are reversed. Order matters between the stretch and the vertical shift: stretching after shifting would multiply the shift too.

@ Precalculus > Functions > Even and odd | d3 | tags: functions
Q: Is $f(x) = {{coef(a,'x^3')}} {{term(b,'x')}}$ even, odd or neither? Justify your answer.
A: Odd, because $f(-x) = -f(x)$.
S: Test by substituting $-x$: $f(-x) = {{a}}(-x)^3 {{signed(b)}}(-x) = -{{a}}x^3 {{term(-b,'x')}} = -\left({{coef(a,'x^3')}} {{term(b,'x')}}\right) = -f(x)$. Every term has an odd power, so every term flips sign, making the whole function odd — its graph has rotational symmetry of order two about the origin. An even function would satisfy $f(-x) = f(x)$ and be built from even powers only, with reflective symmetry in the $y$-axis. A function with both kinds of term is neither.

V: b = int -9..9 except 0
